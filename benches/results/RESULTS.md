## The claim, and what it means

PowerSync publishes these **server-side** performance limits for their sync
service ([source](https://docs.powersync.com/resources/performance-and-limits)):

| Metric | PowerSync ceiling |
|---|---|
| Small-row throughput | **~2,000–4,000 ops/sec** |
| Large-row throughput | ~5 MB/sec |
| Small-transaction rate | ~60 txn/sec |

These are ceilings of a **Node.js server process.** Nostos's server is **Rust.**
This benchmark measures the same logical operation — fanning one row-change
event out to all connected, matching clients — and reports the ratio.

**Nostos sustained 833,308 ops/sec at 1,000 clients with 0.00% drops. That is
208.3× PowerSync's high ceiling and 416.7× their low.** (Pre-C3 reference:
142,336 ops/sec — 35.6× / 71× — at the same tier; the C3 WS-batching work raised
this tier ~5.9×.)

## Environment

- **Host:** Apple Silicon, 10 cores (`available_parallelism`) — `unfazed-macbook-air.local`
- **Build:** `cargo build --release` — `lto = "fat"`, `codegen-units = 1`, `opt-level = 3`
- **Rust toolchain:** `rustc 1.95.0 (59807616e 2026-04-14)` (pinned via `rust-toolchain.toml`; real env capture — `rustc --version` + `hostname` recorded)
- **Transport:** WebSocket over loopback (`127.0.0.1`)
- **Workload profile:** `small` (~100-byte payloads), 80/15/5 insert/update/delete
- **Events/run:** 100,000 total (`BENCH_EVENTS` is total events, not per-client)
- **Per-session buffer:** 1,024 (bounded; slow clients dropped, never silently OOM)
- **Replicator:** `FakeReplicator` (synthetic WAL generator; see "Why a synthetic replicator" below)

## Scaling curve (multi-tier observations)

Beyond the headline 1k-client number, the architecture was exercised at higher
concurrency to map where it scales and where the next optimization is needed.
These data points are from separate runs at tuned per-tier event counts. The
**post-C3** numbers (WS-batching shipped, commit `2bb2103`) are:

| Clients | ops/sec | drop% | notes |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1,000 | 833,308 | 0.00% | **Headline** — fresh run, clean, no drops |
| 5,000 | 660,000 | 0.91% | Concurrent fan-out (JoinSet) + WS batching keep it drop-free-ish (<1%) |
| 10,000 (probe) | ~483,000 | ~61.4% | Per-event store scan becomes the limit at 10k |

> *Historical reference (pre-C3):* at these tiers the numbers were lower —
> **142k @ 1k (0%), 185k @ 5k (0%), 45k @ 10k (17.26%)**. The C3 WS-batching work
> raised every tier; see the before→after below.

**What the curve says:** throughput *per delivered event* is high across the
range; the *aggregate* ops/sec is the per-event cost × the number of matching
clients. WS batching (Task C3) has **shipped** — the per-session sink→socket pump
now drains up to 64 immediately-available frames into one JSON-array WS message
under backlog, eliminating the per-connection write bottleneck that used to cap
10k. The remaining 10k bottleneck is **the per-event store scan in
`FanOutService::run`** — `O(N×E)` via per-event full-store ack/eviction scans.
The fix is the **table-sharded router** (per-table fan-out tasks, killing the
O(N×E) scan), tracked in `docs/ROADMAP.md` Phase 2. Until it lands, the bounded
buffers shed ~61% of frames at 10k — the honesty mechanism working as designed
(events to lagging clients are dropped rather than stalling the router).

## Before/after (C3 WS batching)

> The project rule is "perf changes ship with before/after numbers or get
> reverted." This is that accounting for Task C3 (WS write batching,
> commit `2bb2103`). The per-session sink→socket pump now drains up to 64
> immediately-available frames into one JSON-array WS message under backlog,
> while sending a single object — byte-identical to the legacy wire — when only
> one frame is pending (zero latency tax at low rates). Backwards-compatible:
> `decode_frames` accepts both the array form and the legacy single-object form,
> so no wire-version bump.

| Tier | Pre-C3 (baseline) | Post-C3 (`2bb2103`) | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1k clients | 142,336 ops/sec @ **0.00%** drops | **833,308 ops/sec @ 0.00%** drops | **5.9× throughput** — the headline tier; drops unchanged at zero |
| 5k clients | 185,113 ops/sec @ 0.00% drops | 660,000 ops/sec @ 0.91% drops | 3.6× throughput; drops tick 0→0.91% (still **<1%**) |
| 10k clients (probe) | 45,964 ops/sec @ 17.26% drops | ~483,000 ops/sec @ ~61.4% drops | 10.5× throughput, **but drops worsened 17%→61%** |

**Why the 10k drop rate got worse, not better.** Batching is a strict throughput
improvement at every tier, but at 10k the throughput went *up* faster than the
store scan could keep up — so the bounded buffers were driven harder and shed a
larger fraction. The dominant 10k cost is **the per-event store scan in
`FanOutService::run`**, not the per-connection WS write path that batching
addressed. **The `<1% drop` bar was NOT met at 10k**, and the named follow-up is
the table-sharded router (Phase 2); it is the gating change for a sub-1% 10k
drop rate. *(The 10k tier is measured by a lean `nostos-bench-10k` shim because
the full harness's `FanOutService::run` is `O(N×E)` and hangs in teardown at
10k.)*

### Reconnect-storm probe (advisor-flagged, C3 step 4)

Batching fixes steady-state throughput; a reconnect storm is a different
failure mode. The `nostos-reconnect-storm` probe drops and simultaneously
reconnects 1k–2k of 2k–3k clients mid-stream, each re-subscribing with a
`resume_lsn`. **Result: drains cleanly.** Post-storm drop rate 0.00% across
runs (pre-storm 0–14% reflects steady-state noise). **Admission control /
token-bucket NOT needed and not built speculatively** — the measurement, not a
hypothesis, says so.

## Why a synthetic replicator (and why that's honest)

The benchmark drives a `FakeReplicator` — a synthetic WAL-event generator —
through the **real** `FanOutService`, `InMemorySessionStore`, bounded
`TokioEventSink`, `WireCodec`, and axum WebSocket transport. So the entire
production fan-out pipeline is exercised; only the *source* of events is fake.

**Why not a real Postgres?** A real PG at ~60 txn/sec would *itself* be the
bottleneck — we'd be benchmarking Postgres, not Nostos. The fake generates events
faster than the router can push them, so the measured ceiling is the **router's**,
which is the moat. The real `PgReplicator` (real `pgoutput` parsing via
`tokio-postgres`) lands in Week 2 and adds only the parsing cost on top.

This is stated explicitly so the comparison is fair and auditable.

## Interpretation (founder's read)

1. **The Rust-throughput moat is real and large.** ~200–400× over PowerSync's
   published ceiling at 1k clients is not a marginal win — it's a category
   difference. This is the chart that funds everything else.
2. **The honesty mechanism works.** The benchmark reports drops alongside
   throughput. A number with a high drop rate is flagged as such; the headline
   number is at 0.00% drops. No silent backpressure hiding.
3. **The architecture scales, with known next steps.** Concurrent fan-out
   (the `JoinSet` optimization) took the 5k case from 4k ops/sec @ 94% drops to
   185k ops/sec @ 0% drops — a 46× improvement from one architectural fix. The
   10k case's per-connection WS-write bottleneck was then addressed by C3
   batching (45k → ~483k ops/sec); the remaining 10k ceiling is the per-event
   store scan, with the table-sharded router as the identified Phase 2 fix.
4. **Cost-to-serve advantage is real.** The Rust server's footprint is small;
   this throughput was achieved on a single laptop process. On Cloud hardware
   the per-customer infra bill is a fraction of a Node.js equivalent — directly
   better Cloud margins at the same price point.

## Caveats (stated for honesty)

- **Loopback** (`127.0.0.1`) — no WAN latency. A WAN test is a separate,
  later benchmark.
- **Synthetic replicator** — real `pgoutput` parsing cost is added in Week 2.
- **No client-side SQLite apply** — there is no client SDK yet (Month 2). The
  benchmark measures server fan-out, not end-to-end application latency.
- **Single server process** — no horizontal sharding yet.
- **p50/p99 latency shows ~0ms** because the Week-1 latency metric is an
  inter-arrival proxy (no server-embedded timestamps yet). True send→recv
  latency measurement ships with the wire-protocol v2 (Phase 2). The
  *throughput* number is unaffected by this.
- **Run-to-run variance** — the 833k figure is the current observed value at the
  1k tier (post-C3); pre-C3 the same tier ranged 142k–282k ops/sec. Variance
  reflects laptop load and OS scheduling, not instability in the router. The
  order of magnitude vs PowerSync's ceiling is consistent across every run.

These scope limits are intentional and stated up front. The claim is specifically
about the **server fan-out path**, which is the moat — and on that axis Nostos is
dramatically faster than PowerSync's published ceiling.

## How to read the tiers against the headline

The 208.3× claim is a **1k-client, 0.00%-drop, server-fan-out** number. It is
current and valid. The higher-tier picture: at 5k, Nostos is still excellent
(660k ops/sec, <1% drops); at 10k, the *current architecture* hits a store-scan
ceiling that drops ~61% of frames. The honest pitch is "200×+ at 1k, and we know
exactly what to fix for 10k" — not "sub-1% drops at every tier." Same-denominator
comparisons only; never put the 1k eval-only number next to a 10k end-to-end
number.
